## IDENTIFICATION and REASON FOR REFERRAL

Mr. Bowen is a 34-year-old, Caucasian male who was seen for a neuropsychological evaluation in our office. History is remarkable for social communication difficulties, subtle repetitive and rhythmic motor movements, cognitive rigidity, and social discomfort. Symptoms have been present since childhood and have persisted, leading to suspicions regarding possible autism spectrum disorder (ASD). Thus, the following evaluation was requested by Mr. Bowen at the recommendation of his therapist to provide some diagnostic clarity and appropriate treatment recommendations.

## BACKGROUND INFORMATION

Background information was obtained from a number of sources; Mr. Bowen was the primary source of historical information, though he had his mother complete a developmental history packet provided by our office prior to his intake. Following his intake, I also consulted with his therapist and his husband as a means to gather collateral information.

### Developmental History:

Mr. Bowen was born at term and without complications, though I was not given additional information regarding his gestation or delivery. He is one of five children. Early temperament was unremarkable.

Per his mother, language development was precocious, as he was telling stories by 12 months and knew his letters before age 2. Mr. Bowen does not recall any echoed or scripted speech when younger, nor did his mother indicate any in her responses. She did describe his language and communication as “linear,” however. Mr. Bowen does not recall any issues regarding fine or gross motor functioning, nor were any indicated by his mother. He has never received any developmental services.

### Medical History:

Visible indications of vitiligo began to present themselves somewhere around middle school, although he has never received a formal diagnosis. The progression of this suspected condition has slowed within the last 5 to 10 years. Medical history is remarkable for occasional precordial catch occurring approximately twice annually which Mr. Bowen correlates with stress. Nearsightedness is corrected with lenses; there are no concerns regarding hearing. Mr. Bowen does not currently experience any problems with sleep onset or maintenance. Position-dependent snoring occurs nightly, and he does not endorse any other indications of sleep apnea. Mr. Bowen reports feeling well rested unless experiencing an episode of depression.

Current medication is limited to fluoxetine 20 mg for the past year, which he was also prescribed approximately 10 years ago, and PrEP. He has always found fluoxetine to be helpful, and does not report any side effects.

Mr. Bowen drinks alcohol twice weekly, consuming between one and five drinks per sitting. He uses marijuana daily as a means to self-medicate, with his current form of ingestion being edibles. He does not describe any negative effects of consequences of substance use.

### Educational and Occupational History:

Mr. Bowen was homeschooled until 2nd grade and then again in 3rd grade. He spent much of his childhood education enrolled in gifted programs, and following high school, went on to receive a Bachelor’s Degree in Computer Science. He began struggling to finish assignments halfway through his sophomore year of college, and ultimately withdrew from classes and moved out of state. He describes his depression being at its peak at that time, which he now attributes to possible “autistic shutdown.” Mr. Bowen had a programming internship during his sophomore year and found himself often overly engaged with tasks associated with this internship, as he found it to be more interesting than classes. While his educational history was otherwise unremarkable generally, he reports a longstanding history of difficulties managing the social component of school.

Mr. Bowen and his husband own and operate their own business. They have 11 employees, all of whom work virtually. As such, Mr. Bowen describes frequent “parallel interactions” as well as communication via technology. Mr. Bowen has worked hard to make his work environment a safe space, and as a means to do so, does not take external sales calls and communicates only through e-mail.

### Social and Behavioral History:

According to his mother, Mr. Bowen was very socially interested when younger, and often preferred to interact with older adults. His eye contact was present and “intense” from infancy. Mr. Bowen reports having his first identifiable friend in 1st grade, and recalls first becoming aware of difficulties making friends in 4th grade. He now has one close friend outside of his spouse, and describes always feeling uncomfortable interacting even with those with whom he is close and familiar. According to both Mr. Bowen’s husband and therapist, Mr. Bowen can be fairly blunt, struggles to read social cues, and his husband feels that Mr. Bowen does not always pick up on social norms. In response, both suspect a fair amount of social anxiety, and feel that Mr. Bowen becomes overwhelmed in social situations. Mr. Bowen has been told that he has high emotional intelligence; however, he feels he struggles to differentiate his emotions from those around him.

Mr. Bowen describes difficulties recognizing appropriate turn-taking during social conversation, while his husband has noticed trouble interpreting nonliteral language and figures of speech. These challenges are recognized by Mr. Bowen, as he describes himself as very literal at times, and reports occasionally struggling to tell if someone is joking.

Mr. Bowen reports periods of “special focus” dating back to childhood, but was unable to provide specifics during intake. Mr. Bowen, his husband, and his therapist all describe subtle yet noticeable repetitive and rhythmic tensing of his muscles and similar finger movements. When recognized, Mr. Bowen finds these complex and rhythmic movements soothing, and has recently noticed that they tend to increase when stressed. He prefers a predictable schedule with “consistent elements,” but has tried to be amenable to change when necessary. Mr. Bowen does describe some rigid or inflexible preferences, most often related to work. Collaterals also describe rigid coping mechanisms and preferences, as well as a tendency to become overwhelmed when Mr. Bowen’s schedule is thrown off or changed.

During the intake, Mr. Bowen struggled to identify any specific sensory sensitivities, as he reported “working hard to be skilled at coping.” Later into the testing experience, he identified some sensory sensitivities related to food which he has addressed by having a private chef cook specific meals for him. According to his husband, Mr. Bowen wears ear plugs pretty frequently to avoid unwanted auditory input. He suspects tactile sensitivity related to shoes, and as such, has specific preferences regarding footwear. Mr. Bowen and his husband both describe Mr. Bowen as being drawn to small objects, including a set of small chopstick rests in the shape of cats which he carries with him and sets next to himself while working.

Mr. Bowen was diagnosed with major depressive disorder in middle school, with symptoms including loss of interest and feeling slowed down. History is remarkable for anxiety, resulting in avoidance of social situations. He has tried ketamine with moderate success, but has found medication to be most helpful with strengthening his “emotional resilience.” He describes emotionally impactful events regarding sexuality dating back to childhood that have had a noticeable impact on his emotional status. I am not aware of any specific history of trauma. Mr. Bowen has been working with therapist Anne Mirza, LCSW, for the last few months. Through his sessions with Ms. Mirza, they have explored the possibility of ASD. In response to these conversations, he has started to view his experiences through a different lens, and now suspects that his history of anxiety and depression might be a secondary consequence of the struggles associated with potential ASD.

### Family History:

Family history is remarkable for strong suspicions of ASD in Mr. Bowen’s father and paternal grandfather. One of Mr. Bowen’s siblings (a sister) has informed Mr. Bowen that she is on the autism spectrum, and another sibling is suspected to also be on the autism spectrum. Giftedness is present throughout his immediate family. Extended family history is unremarkable or unknown.

## VALIDITY and BEHAVIORAL OBSERVATIONS

### Validity:

Performance validity was assessed using a standalone performance validity test (PVT). Mr. Bowen performed adequately on this PVT, resulting in a valid profile. PVTs embedded within objective measures were also deemed valid. This supports basic effort and there being no evidence of active feigning during the current evaluation. Results of symptom validity tests (SVTs) will be discussed below.

### Behavioral Observations:

Mr. Bowen’s willingness to participate in the evaluation process was mediated by his level of frustration and response to ambiguity. Generally, he was very polite, forthcoming, and engaged with the testing process. When confident in his responses, he was positive and quick to respond. As testing progressed, however, he became overly self-critical and critical of the test makers. Specifically, when presented with social based measures, he seemed to focus on uncovering the “algorithm” of the test, and would become very easily frustrated when unable to do so. He would frequently reported answering questions in the way that he believed “test makers would like” and often misinterpreted social-based items negatively or sarcastically. Mr. Bowen relied heavily on his impressive verbal reasoning to work his way through social cognitive problems, which often worked in his favor. Having said that, I was struck by the level of effort and energy he needed to put towards solving these social-based problems, as well as the degree of frustration and emotionality that he would exhibit when presented with ambiguity or when he misinterpreted social themes.

Mr. Bowen’s eye contact was mixed, but often appropriate when made, and he demonstrated a reasonable use of nonverbals. He was subtly formal in his language use, though I did not observe any idiosyncratic speech. Consistent with anecdotal report, I noticed very subtle rhythmic clenching of his jaw and other muscles during conversation, but did not observe any other stimming behavior. Despite his increasing level of frustration and aggravation, Mr. Bowen was able to complete all aspects of testing requested. I want to be clear that frustration and aggression very clearly came from a place of anxiety rather than anger, and when recognized, he was very apologetic and remorseful. The following evaluation appears to be an adequate reflection of his overall level of functioning.

## EVALUATION RESULTS

### Intellectual Functioning:

Mr. Bowen completed the WAIS-IV to assess his overall intellectual functioning, and achieved a superior Full Scale IQ score (SS=124, 95th percentile). A reader can best understand his specific strengths and intrapersonal weaknesses by considering the individual indices. To that end, his verbal skills, as measured by the Verbal Comprehension Index, were very superior (SS=132, 98th percentile). His nonverbal abilities, as measured by the Perceptual Reasoning Index, were superior (SS=123, 94th percentile). Mr. Bowen’s Processing Speed Index was high average (SS=111, 77th percentile). His Working Memory Index was average (SS=105, 63rd percentile), but reflective of an intrapersonal or relative weakness when compared to his impressive general cognitive abilities. Given the discrepancies between indices, I calculated two additional indices: The first is the General Ability Index, which summarizes verbal and nonverbal skills, and is considered a better indicator of one’s cognitive potential when such discrepancies arise. His GAI was very superior (SS=131, 98th percentile). The second index is the Cognitive Proficiency Index, which summarizes working memory and processing speed, and provides insight into how readily one can bring his or her general cognitive skills to bear. Mr. Bowen’s CPI was high average (SS=114, 82nd percentile). Practically speaking, this discrepancy suggests that, while Mr. Bowen presents with gifted general cognitive skills, it might take him a little bit longer than what others might expect of him to access these skills. I will use his scores on the WAIS-IV as a means of comparison to other measures.

### Complex Attention:

ADHD is highly comorbid with ASD, but can also mimic ASD. Mr. Bowen completed the CPT-3, which is a measure of inattention, impulsivity, and vigilance (or one’s ability to remain focused when under-stimulated). He was able to produce a valid profile, and his response style indicates that he emphasized speed over accuracy. Despite this emphasis on speed, he performed very well, with all T scores falling well within normal limits (WNL). As a result, he matched better with a nonclinical population, or individuals his age who do not demonstrate attentional deficits such as those that might be associated with ADHD.

### Social Cognition and Related Functions:

Mr. Bowen, his husband, and his therapist were given multiple rating scales used in the assessment of ASD. Mr. Bowen completed the CAT-Q, which is designed to identify the presence of possible “masking” in those suspected to be on the autism spectrum. His Total Score matched more with a clinical population. While his profile of scores did not suggest a need to compensate for social difficulties, he did indicate a predisposition to mask social challenges, as well as a tendency to “assimilate” or attempt to fit in in social situations, even when uncomfortable. Mr. Bowen completed the AAA, which is a structured clinical interview designed to assess symptoms of high-functioning ASD. This yields two separate scores, the first is the Autism Spectrum Quotient (AQ) which looks at autistic mannerisms, and the second is the Empathy Quotient (EQ), which measures one’s ability to engage in perspective taking. Both scores on the AAA fell WNL (AQ=26; EQ=42). Mr. Bowen, his husband, and his therapist all completed the SRS-2. This is an evidence-based rating scale designed to assess the presence and level of severity of ASD symptomology, including deficits in social communication and interaction (SCI) and the presence of restricted interests and repetitive behaviors (RRB). His therapist left questions related to RRBs incomplete, and as such, I was unable to calculate a Total Score. Her SCI scale was mildly elevated (63T). Mr. Bowen and his husband answered all questions on the SRS-2. Mr. Bowen’s Total Score and SCI scale both fell within the average range; his RRB scale fell right at the clinical threshold (60T). His husband’s Total Score was moderately elevated (69T), as was his SCI scale (71T); his RRB scale was mild (62T). His husband indicated severe deficits in social motivation and, moderate deficits in social communication, and mild deficits in social cognition.

Social cognition and related skills were assessed objectively using multiple measures. Objective test scores are compared to one’s verbal intellect when assessing for high functioning ASD, and I remind readers that Mr. Bowen’s VCI is very superior (SS=132, 98th percentile). While some social cognitive scores do not reach as high as his VCI, having this source of comparison is nonetheless crucial when trying to identify subtle – and possibly “maskable” – challenges.

Mr. Bowen completed the Social Perception domain from the ACS, which assesses one’s ability to appropriately read emotional cues in the faces of others, interpret social perception and prosody of speech, and correctly match faces and voices to intended emotion. His Composite Score on the ACS was average (SS=105, 63rd percentile), though multiple standard deviations lower than the ceiling of this measure and his verbal intellect. Mr. Bowen performed best on the ACS when given multiple sources of input, which he used in order to reason his way through items (i.e., pairs SS=125, 95th percentile; superior). His ability to simply pick up on pitch and tone fell just into the high average range (Prosody SS=110, 75th percentile), while his ability to recognize emotional cues in others was average (Affect Naming SS=105, 63rd percentile).

The ACS is simple in its demands, as many subtests require individuals to identify emotions based on still pictures or simple audio. Therefore, I also had him complete the CAM. The CAM requires individuals to interpret emotional meaning based on short video or audio vignettes more consistent with what one might experience in real world social situations. Due to technical issues, Mr. Bowen was only administered the voice task. His accuracy of 93% matched far better with a nonclinical population (Clinical=71% accuracy; Controls=86% accuracy). He was observed systematically reducing the multiple-choice options during this measure, but struggled to spontaneously offer appropriate organic interpretations, and reported feeling flustered when asked to give a “first instinct” response.

Social language was assessed using portions of the CASL. I chose to use the original CASL, as the ceiling of the test is greater than the CASL-2, which is useful when working with individuals with high intellect. Mr. Bowen’s ability to interpret nonliteral language (SS=123, 94th percentile) and draw inferences from spoken text (SS=128, 97th percentile) were both superior. His ability to respond to increasingly complex social situations using language fell into the borderline impaired range (SS=74, 4th percentile). It was during the CASL that Mr. Bowen began responding to items based on how he believed the test creators would want. He was vocal in his thought process during Nonliteral Language and Inference, which allowed me insight into how he might process social aspects of language. He would often initially interpret all speech as sarcasm, but was able to reason his way through questions accurately, despite putting forth a great deal of effort while doing so. Mr. Bowen became increasingly frustrated during Pragmatic Judgment in particular, as he tended to misinterpret social situations – often negatively – resulting in frustration and progressively negative affect.

Mr. Bowen also completed the CAPs, which is a comprehensive measure designed to assess one’s understanding of social communication in the context of social interaction, including his or her ability to interpret social language, express verbal and nonverbal intent, and to detect and interpret nonverbal cues. Mr. Bowen is technically above the age range for the CAPs (ceiling=18:11). Therefore, his performance was compared to highest age norms. Mr. Bowen’s Composite Score was average (SS=102, 55th percentile). The Pragmatic Judgment Index assesses one’s ability to detect, comprehend, and interpret social routine and social cues. Mr. Bowen’s Pragmatic Judgment Index was low average (SS=86, 18th percentile), and multiple standard deviations lower than what I would have expected of him. The Pragmatic Performance Index assesses one’s ability to use nonverbal means of communication to express emotion and intent. His Pragmatic Performance Index was high average (SS=117, 87th percentile). The Paralinguistic Index assesses one’s ability to detect, interpret, and utilize nonverbal means of communication. His Paralinguistic Index was average (SS=101, 53rd percentile). Mr. Bowen’s greatest difficulty on the CAPs was in reading nonverbal cues, with his performance falling to the cut-off between borderline and mild impairment (SS=70, 2nd percentile). His strength was in his ability to express emotion (SS=120, 91st percentile; superior), with the remainder of the subtests falling between these two extremes. When compared to himself, he demonstrated subtle deficits reading nonverbal cues (SS=100, 50th percentile) and demonstrating awareness of basic social routine (SS=95, 37th percentile).

Mr. Bowen completed additional observation and clinical interview-based measures meant to assess for ASD. These included the ADOS-2 Module 4 and the MIGDAS-2, and his therapist and I each completed the CARS-2-HF. The ADOS-2 is an interactive, semi-structured evaluation that sets up various “presses” to elicit socially appropriate responses and reactions. The MIGDAS-2 is a structured clinical interview that does not offer formal cut scores, but does allow individuals to provide insight into their experiences and challenges. The CARS-2-HF is a clinician-completed observation tool that summarizes observed symptoms into a Total Score.

Mr. Bowen’s eye contact was inconsistent, but appropriate when made. He was very articulate in his responding – even when frustrated – with his language use often matching his verbal intellect. Having said that, there was a slight formality to his speech, and he struggled at times to engage in reciprocal conversation. His use of empathic and emotional gestures was appropriate and consistent, though his use of descriptive or conventional gestures was somewhat lacking. As mentioned in the behavioral observations, I observed very subtle rhythmic clenching of his jaw and other muscles, but did not observe any other overt stereotyped body movements. He used a wide range of emotional expressions; however, at times, these seemed somewhat over the top relative to the provocation, making it difficult to accurately gauge his emotional state. During the course of the evaluation, Mr. Bowen presented as fairly cognitively rigid, which carried over to the ADOS-2 and CARS-2. While there was a reasonable degree of anxiety behind this rigidity, his anxiety seemed to be in response to being pressed to deviate from his rigid expectations, rather than a motivating factor towards his difficulty adjusting or transitioning. His Total Score on the ADOS-2 fell below the cut-off for a “classic” autism, but above the cut-off for high-functioning ASD (i.e., autism spectrum). On the CARS-2-HF, his therapist and I both produced identical total scores, which were suggestive of mild to moderate symptoms of ASD.

The MIGDAS-2 was completed near the end of the evaluation, at which point, Mr. Bowen seemed to be emotionally and mentally exhausted. As mentioned, he had put forth a great deal of effort and energy toward “figuring out the algorithm” for all objective social measures, and continued to do so during the MIGDAS-2. Unfortunately, however, the MIGDAS-2 does not really have much of an “algorithm,” as questions are simply asking individuals to provide insight into their own experiences. Mr. Bowen frequently indicated that the questions asked of him during the MIGDAS-2 were too vague or ambiguous, and were missing crucial context needed in order to be answered fully. In response, he appeared to shut down during the MIGDAS-2, and struggled to provide answers to most questions. Having said that, he did indicate some sensory sensitivity related to food, sounds, and clothing, with his descriptions of sensory sensitivity being consistent with what his husband had reported during my clinical interview with his husband. He discussed his discomfort when in crowds or social situations, often in response to concerns about failing to meet the expectations of “normative socialization.” Mr. Bowen discussed a preference for routines in certain situations, but seemed to show mixed insight regarding his response to change or transition. Finally, he discussed feeling safest with his husband and his teammates, as it sounds like he has been able to create a comfortable working environment for himself that meets his needs.

### Psychological Functioning:

Psychological functioning was assessed using multiple measures. Mr. Bowen completed the PAI. Results of embedded SVTs would suggest a tendency to portray himself as being relatively free of common shortcomings and reluctant to recognize minor faults. SVTs would suggest a level of defensiveness, as well as a tendency to repress undesirable characteristics. Conversely, his PAI profile would suggest endorsement of problems of greater intensity in specific areas, including sensation-seeking behaviors and poor interpersonal rapport. These potential “over endorsements” are not surprising, however, given the nature of his referral question. In terms of his actual scores, all full scale scores fell well within normal limits, as did most subscale profile scores. The exception to these were social detachment (69T) and stimulus seeking (67T). In the absence of other elevations, these endorsements would suggest difficulties connecting emotionally with others or feeling comfortable in social experiences, as well as a need to seek out excitement or stimulus from the environment.

Mr. Bowen completed the MAQ, which is designed to explore specific triggers for anxious distress. His lone clinical elevation was for social phobia (79T), suggesting a significant degree of social anxiety, which he was able to discuss during the testing and feedback sessions.

## IMPRESSIONS

I spent a good deal of time talking with Mr. Bowen, his husband, and his therapist about the results of testing. Mr. Bowen is an intelligent man who reports putting forth significant effort and energy to overcome challenges related to social functioning including social cognition and communication. Despite his high intelligence, he produced scores on objective measures of social cognition that were multiple standard deviations below what I would have expected for him, and at times, well below what would be expected in the general population. His profile suggests the presence of some **social anxiety (ICD-10=F40.11)**; however, social anxiety alone does not account for the severity of his symptoms, nor does it take into consideration the developmental trajectory of these symptoms. Likewise, the amount of effort he has to put in to correct these challenges and the exhaustion he reports experiencing (and demonstrated during testing) in response to addressing social difficulty is beyond what would be expected in an individual with social anxiety and/or high intelligence alone. Given this, his symptoms are best attributed to **autism spectrum disorder level 1 (ICD-10=F84.0)**, and more specifically, a symptom presentation consistent with what used to be considered Asperger’s disorder in the previous nomenclature.

## RECOMMENDATIONS

Based on the results of the neuropsychological evaluation and my conversation with Mr. Bowen and his husband, I would offer the following recommendations:

1. I recommend Mr. Bowen share a copy of this report with his primary care physician to ensure that his medical records are kept up-to-date. His profile suggests that he might benefit from an antianxiety medication to help ameliorate some of his anxious distress, with one goal being that if the severity of his distress is reduced, he might be more readily able to “take in” interventions to help address his symptoms. I will, of course, defer decisions regarding medication to the discretion of Mr. Bowen and his PCP, and am happy to answer any questions his physician might have regarding the results of this evaluation.
2. I will send a copy of this report to Anne Mirza, LCSW, so that results can be incorporated into treatment planning. As I mentioned to Ms. Mirza, I think Mr. Bowen would benefit from intervention that focuses on social cognition including social communication, reading emotional cues in others, and interpreting and utilizing nonverbal means of communication. Additionally, while it sounds like Mr. Bowen has skillfully created an environment that meets his needs, I do have concerns regarding his ability to manage distress when taken out of his “safe space.” Therefore, he will likely benefit from working on frustration tolerance.
   1. I also discussed with Mr. Bowen the concept of “masking” and when masking is adaptive versus maladaptive. While he should never feel the need to not be himself, it is also important to be able to recognize when accommodation or coping skills are helpful and/or necessary. For example, while he should not feel the need to make eye contact with “everyone all the time,” eye contact does serve a purpose in certain situations, as it allows the other individual to know that you are paying attention and you care about what they have to say.
3. Mr. Bowen and his family would benefit from exploring the following resources:
   1. _The Complete Guide to Asperger’s Syndrome_ by Tony Attwood
   2. _Living Well on the Spectrum: How to Use Your Strengths to Meet the Challenges of Asperger’s Syndrome/High-Functioning Autism_ by Valerie L. Gaus, PhD
   3. _The Other Half of Asperger’s Syndrome, Second Edition_ by Maxine Aston
   4. _Asperger’s and Adulthood: A Guide to Working, Loving, and Living_ by Blyth Grossberg, PsyD
   5. _The Autistic Brain: Helping Different Kinds of Minds Succeed_ by Temple Grandin and Richard Panek
4. He might want to look into social groups or support groups for adults with high functioning autism. For example, Chicago Asperger Adults and Friends is a local support group for adults who are on the spectrum. Likewise, Center for Autism and Related Disorders (CARD) are for social skills groups for individuals of all ages (872-810-9312).
5. **LifeSpeed Behavioral Support Services** (312-404-7225) offers support and other targeted services for those high functioning on the spectrum, and designs intervention to meet the unique goals of the individual.
6. Mr. Bowen might wish to consider neurofeedback to address emotional control and anxiety. if this is something he is interested in pursuing within our office, I am happy to facilitate that process for him.

I am happy to augment or amend any of the recommendations above based on response to intervention or in the event that additional accommodations or interventions are deemed necessary. I appreciate the opportunity to participate in Mr. Bowen’s care.
